# Ten simple rules for structuring scientific papers

This is a synthesis of Brett Mensh and Konrad Kording's *Ten simple rules for
structuring papers* (2017). The paper treats scientific writing as a problem of
structure: a manuscript should make its central contribution readable,
credible, and memorable to people who do not already know the work.

Source: [Mensh and Kording, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5619685/),
*PLOS Computational Biology* 13(9): e1005619.

## General principles

### 1. Build the paper around one contribution

A paper may contain several findings, but they should support one main message
rather than compete for attention. That message can be multifaceted if the
connection among its parts is clear. State it in the title, which both tells
readers what matters and helps authors decide what belongs in the manuscript.
The goal is the simplest claim the evidence supports, not an oversimplified
one.

### 2. Write for readers who do not know the work

Authors are too familiar with their own research to experience it as new
readers do. Treat each part of the paper as a design problem: decide what the
reader needs to understand, then make that understanding require as little
effort as possible. Explain technical terms, limit abbreviations, and avoid
leaving many unresolved ideas in the reader's working memory.

### 3. Use context, content, and conclusion

Organize ideas as context–content–conclusion:

- Context explains why the material matters.
- Content presents the evidence or reasoning.
- Conclusion states what the material establishes.

This pattern works at several scales. Across the paper, the introduction
provides context, the results provide content, and the discussion develops the
conclusion. Within a paragraph, the opening frames a topic or question, the
middle develops it, and the ending gives the takeaway.

The final paper should follow the logic of its claim, not the chronology of the
research. Readers need the soundest route from question to conclusion, not a
record of every false start.

### 4. Maintain a continuous, parallel flow

Develop each secondary idea in one place instead of repeatedly switching
between topics. Present comparable arguments or results in comparable forms
and in a consistent order. Use the same term for the same concept; unnecessary
synonyms can suggest distinctions that do not exist.

## The major sections

### 5. Make the abstract a complete story

The abstract must carry the whole argument in miniature because it is often the
only part people read. Begin with the broad field, narrow to the unresolved gap
and why it matters, identify the approach, summarize the principal results,
and conclude with the answer and its broader significance. This
broad–narrow–broad structure gives results enough context to be meaningful.

### 6. Use the introduction to establish importance

Move from a broad problem to the specific gap addressed by the paper.
Successive paragraphs can narrow from field, to subfield, to the exact
unanswered question. Each paragraph should orient the reader, explain what is
known, and end with the relevant unknown.

Include literature insofar as it establishes the gap rather than attempting a
comprehensive review. Once the gap is clear, the final paragraph should
summarize what the paper did and found, more specifically than the abstract and
without repeating the context.

### 7. Present results as a chain of supported claims

Order results by the logic of the argument rather than by when the work was
done. Express that logic as a sequence of declarative statements that can
become subsection headings, figure titles, or opening sentences. Each result
should support the next step toward the central contribution.

Figures carry much of the evidence and are often read immediately after the
abstract. Their titles should state the supported conclusion, while their
legends explain how the evidence was produced.

The first results paragraph commonly summarizes the approach and essential
methods. Later paragraphs should frame a question, present the relevant
evidence and reasoning, and answer the question. This structure lets readers
locate and inspect each inferential step.

### 8. Use the discussion to calibrate the contribution

Explain how the findings address the original gap, what limits their
interpretation, and what they change or enable. The opening paragraph should
summarize the main findings and their meaning. Later paragraphs can examine
strengths and weaknesses in relation to prior work, identify unresolved
questions, and broaden toward future implications.

## Writing and revision

### 9. Spend effort where readers place attention

Make the logic connecting evidence to claim explicit while the research is
still developing. During writing, devote disproportionate attention to the
title, abstract, and figures because they reach the most readers.

Outline before polishing prose. One informal sentence per planned paragraph
makes each paragraph's purpose visible and exposes structural problems early.
The results and their logical relationships are often the best place to begin.

### 10. Revise the story through feedback

Improve the overall story, outline, and sentences together. Do not preserve
weak prose merely because it already exists; replacing a paragraph or
rebuilding the structure may be more effective than accumulating local edits.

Reader feedback diagnoses problems at different scales. Broad confusion or
indifference suggests that the central story is unclear, while a precise
objection often reveals a missing step in a local argument. If an author cannot
explain the outline quickly, or a colleague cannot recover the main
contribution, the story needs further distillation.

## Scope

These rules describe the structure of a scientific manuscript, not every
aspect of grammar, style, creativity, or collaboration. They are defaults
rather than absolute laws: departures are most effective when the writer first
understands what the conventional structure does for the reader.
